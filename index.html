01 — PACIFIC IS CHANGING

Thermal expansion and shifting sea surface patterns set the baseline for Pacific island vulnerability.

Ocean waters absorb over 90% of the excess heat trapped in the Earth system. Across the Pacific basin, gradual increases in sea surface temperature and upper-ocean heat content alter local marine dynamics and atmosphere-ocean energy exchanges.

Understanding this evolving thermal baseline provides essential context for analyzing storm frequency, track shifts, and long-term ecosystem exposure.

Fig 1.1 — Ocean thermal indicators and surface anomaly distributions across the Pacific basin.

A changing environment is only part of the story.

Cyclones occur within this environmental backdrop — but exposure is not distributed evenly across populations.

02 — THE HAZARD

Trajectory, intensity, and frequency: mapping cyclone pathways across the basin.

Tropical cyclones are complex atmospheric phenomena fueled by ocean heat, atmospheric pressure gradients, and regional wind shear. While ocean energy enables storm development, historical pathways demonstrate pronounced geographic clustering.

Tracking historical storm paths over time reveals distinct zones of recurring hazard, where specific island territories encounter severe storm conditions with elevated statistical regularity.

Fig 2 — Cyclones, and density distribution across Pacific island territories.

But frequency is only part of the story.

The true measure of a storm’s severity lies in where its path intersects human settlement, infrastructure, and local economic resilience •

03 — THE IMPACTS

Disproportionate losses: how geographic exposure translates into human and economic cost.

When a cyclone impacts an island territory, losses extend far beyond immediate physical damage. Geographic isolation, coastal infrastructure concentration, and limited capital reserves compound the social and economic disruption.

Comparing physical hazard metrics against socioeconomic vulnerability reveals a clear inequality: communities experiencing the highest relative economic losses often possess the smallest resource buffers to recover before the next event.
